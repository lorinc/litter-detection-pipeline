Litter detection demo

A small detector (YOLO11s or YOLO11n) finds cigarette butts on pavement. It runs entirely in your browser, on your GPU where the browser supports WebGPU; photos are never uploaded anywhere.

What photos it is made for

The model was trained on one kind of photo. The closer a photo is to it, the better the detection; every difference below lowers accuracy, often a lot.

  • Pavement seen from low down: a phone held about 50 cm above the ground (the height of a small robot's camera), tilted down at the ground in front, so the ground fills most of the frame.
  • Full resolution, landscape: the original photo from the phone camera (about 4000×1800 px or more), not a screenshot or a copy shrunk by a messaging app. The model finds butts that cover roughly 30–250 pixels along their length.
  • Daylight, dry ground: sun or shade. No night, rain or flash.
  • City pavement: tiles, brick, asphalt, kerbs, drains. Grass, gravel, sand and leaf litter were not in training.
  • Sharp: hold still; motion blur hides small butts.

Photos taken standing up (butts too small in pixels), extreme close-ups, other camera heights, other cities and other light all move away from what the model has seen. Even within its own kind of photo, close-ups of one or two butts are its weakest case.

How good is it?

Three models are offered, all trained on the same 87 photos from one 22-minute walk in Valencia, Spain, with one phone. YOLO11s first is the first version. YOLO11s adjusted, the default, is the same model size also trained on tiles of things that look like butts but are not, such as cracks, gum spots and leaves: the places where OWLv2, an off-the-shelf, generic vision model asked in words to find cigarette butts, found one that a person then rejected. YOLO11n fast is the adjusted training on the nano model, a quarter of the size.

  • On the 19 held-out photos of the walk (the examples are four of them), butts found and false alarms per photo, counting boxes from each colour band up:
    • Green only (78% and up): YOLO11s adjusted 74% (34 of 46), 0.05; YOLO11n fast 72% (33), 0; YOLO11s first 67% (31), 0.26.
    • Green and orange (65% and up): adjusted 78% (36), 0.16; fast 74% (34), 0.11; first 80% (37), 0.68.
    • All boxes, red included: adjusted 89% (41), 9.6; fast 87% (40), 7.4; first 100% (46), 21.
    The first version's saved checkpoint was the one that scored best on these same photos, which flatters it.
  • Over all 106 photos (five-fold cross-validation: each photo scored by a model that did not train on it), counting boxes scoring 25% and up: the adjustment cut false alarms from 1.85 to 1.26 per photo, a real difference; recall went from 86% to 84%, a difference inside noise. The fast model found 81% with 1.01 false alarms per photo: fewer false alarms than YOLO11s adjusted (real), recall inside noise.
  • Compared at equal recall instead of an equal score cut-off (80%, 85% and 88%), no two training recipes differ by more than noise: most of the drop in false alarms at 25% comes from lower scores overall. The fast model reaches at most 89% recall even counting every box, against 92% for YOLO11s adjusted.
  • Speed: in this page on one laptop's CPU, a 50-tile photo took 14 s with YOLO11n fast against 39 s with YOLO11s adjusted; the GPU path has not been timed.

The models' scores are not on the same scale, so the same score means a different level of confidence for each. All these photos share streets, light and phone with the training set, so expect lower accuracy on your own photos. Full results: cross-validation write-up, training write-up. Dataset: Kaggle (CC BY 4.0).
