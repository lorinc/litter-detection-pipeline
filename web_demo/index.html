Litter detection demo

A small detector (YOLO11s) finds cigarette butts on pavement. It runs entirely in your browser, on your GPU where the browser supports WebGPU; photos are never uploaded anywhere.

What photos it is made for

The model was trained on one kind of photo. The closer a photo is to it, the better the detection; every difference below lowers accuracy, often a lot.

  • Pavement seen from low down: a phone held about 50 cm above the ground (the height of a small robot's camera), tilted down at the ground in front, so the ground fills most of the frame.
  • Full resolution, landscape: the original photo from the phone camera (about 4000×1800 px or more), not a screenshot or a copy shrunk by a messaging app. The model finds butts that cover roughly 30–250 pixels along their length.
  • Daylight, dry ground: sun or shade. No night, rain or flash.
  • City pavement: tiles, brick, asphalt, kerbs, drains. Grass, gravel, sand and leaf litter were not in training.
  • Sharp: hold still; motion blur hides small butts.

Photos taken standing up (butts too small in pixels), extreme close-ups, other camera heights, other cities and other light all move away from what the model has seen. Even within its own kind of photo, close-ups of one or two butts are its weakest case.

How good is it?

Two models are offered. Both are YOLO11s, trained on the same 87 photos from one 22-minute walk in Valencia, Spain, with one phone. 04 Hard negatives (fold 0), the default, was also trained on tiles of things that look like butts but are not, such as cracks, gum spots and leaves. 02 Maybe masked is the earlier model, trained without them. The number is the run's place in the sequence of training runs.

  • On the 19 held-out photos of the walk (the examples are four of them), at threshold 0.25: hard negatives found 83% of butts (38 of 46) with 0.84 false alarms per photo; 02 found 89% (41 of 46) with 2.4. 02's saved checkpoint was the one that scored best on these same photos, which flatters it.
  • Over all 106 photos (five-fold cross-validation: each photo scored by a model that did not train on it), at threshold 0.25: adding hard negatives to 02's recipe cut false alarms from 1.9 to 1.3 per photo, a real difference; recall went from 86% to 84%, a difference inside noise.
  • Compared at equal recall instead of equal threshold, the two recipes are about equal between 75% and 86% recall: part of the drop in false alarms at 0.25 comes from the hard-negative model giving lower scores overall. At high recall hard negatives do better: at 88% recall, 2.3 false alarms per photo against 3.2; at 90%, 2.6 against 4.7. These last figures are read from the curves and have no error margin.

The two models' scores are not on the same scale, so one threshold means a different trade-off for each. All these photos share streets, light and phone with the training set, so expect lower accuracy on your own photos. Full results: cross-validation write-up, training write-up. Dataset: Kaggle (CC BY 4.0).
